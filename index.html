India's high frequency data update:
Manufacturing PMI eased to its lowest level since August 2021, while GST collections remained robust, supported by a sharp rise in import revenue. Core-sector output and industrial production rebounded, and credit growth accelerated marginally. However, inflation edged higher-CPI rose to 4.82% YoY in August from 4.45% in July, with food inflation at 5.95%- and the merchandise trade deficit widened to $31.98 billion in July, before narrowing to $26.86 billion in August.
Purchasing Managers’ Index Manufacturing PMI:
India's Manufacturing PMI rose to 55.1 in September 2026 from 52.8 in August, revised down from a preliminary estimate of 55.7, but marking one of the strongest improvements in the sector's health in seven months. New orders increased at their fastest pace in seven months, while output growth accelerated to a four-month high, supported by firmer demand for electronic, food, pharmaceutical and textile products. New export orders also expanded at a quicker pace, with stronger demand from Brazil, Europe, the UAE and the US.

Inflation:
For August 2026, the Consumer Price Index (CPI) inflation rate rose YoY to (+4.82%), up from (+4.45%) in July 2026. Food inflation accelerated further to (+5.95%) YoY, compared with (+5.52%) in July. The Wholesale Price Index (WPI) inflation in August 2026 was at around (+9.92%) YoY, compared with (+9.78%) YoY in July 2026. The higher inflation rate in August 2026 was primarily driven by food prices, fuel and power, and broad-based increases across most CPI categories; notably, onion inflation rose sharply to 48.27% YoY, while fuel and power inflation at the wholesale level increased to 22.93% YoY. Deflationary trends were observed in items such as tomatoes, potatoes, and some primary articles, although these were insufficient to offset the broader inflationary pressure.

Consequently, India’s merchandise trade deficit narrowed to $26.86 billion in August 2026, from around $27.2 billion in August 2025 and $31.98 billion in July 2026.

Market View
Global macro conditions remain volatile, with crude oil prices, interest rates, commodities and currencies continue to remain elevated.
On the domestic front, trends remain encouraging. Consumption has recovered following the GST rate changes, while government-led investment remains supportive and private-sector capex continues to improve from a relatively low base.
Corporate earnings have also shown signs of improvement, with revenue and earnings growth moving into double digits. Q1 FY 27 quarterly results were ahead of expectations, providing greater confidence in the earnings recovery.
FII selling had started to moderate after significant outflows in the first seven months, but renewed geopolitical tensions and higher crude prices may keep foreign flows volatile. Foreign Currency Non Resident (FCNR) flows have provided some support to the currency, though they do not address the structural impact of higher energy costs.
Domestic liquidity remains an important support for equities, with SIP flows continuing at healthy levels. However, a prolonged period of market weakness could have some impact on the pace of incremental flows.
At the same time, strong primary-market activity through IPOs and offers for sale is absorbing significant liquidity. Continued supply of new paper could keep a lid on the pace of market recovery in the near term.
Valuations have become more reasonable following the time correction in Indian equities, particularly in large caps. This has potentially improved the risk-reward compared with the elevated valuations seen earlier.
Going forward, earnings growth may be an important driver of market returns, particularly as valuations have become more reasonable and the market navigates external uncertainties around crude oil, geopolitics and global interest rates.
India remains vulnerable to elevated crude oil prices given its dependence on energy imports. A sustained rise in crude could create a dual impact through pressure on the currency as well as corporate earnings.
Overall, while near-term volatility could persist, improving domestic earnings, resilient consumption and investment trends, healthy domestic flows and more reasonable valuations may remain supportive. We might remain constructive on Indian equities from a medium- to long-term perspective.
Asset allocation in line with the risk appetite of the investor is an important tool to navigate any unanticipated volatility. Accordingly, Large Cap & Large Cap oriented diversified strategies along with hybrid funds might appear to be better placed on risk-reward basis, while Mid/Small cap allocation may be considered in a staggered manner through systematic investment with a long-term view.
Gold & Silver Outlook
Gold and silver have both corrected from recent highs after a strong rally, but the medium to long term investment case may remain constructive. Our view is that volatility may remain elevated at least until the year end. Higher interest rates and bond yields may remain a headwind, while portfolio diversification, hedging demand, fiscal concerns and tight physical markets may provide support.
For gold, the key structural development is its resilience despite higher global bond yields. Traditionally, rising real yields have been negative for gold, but this relationship has weakened since the pandemic. One explanation is that higher yields are increasingly reflecting higher term premiums and concerns around fiscal sustainability, rather than only stronger growth or tighter monetary policy. With global debt continuing to rise and limited visibility on fiscal consolidation, gold's role as an asset without direct credit risk becomes increasingly relevant.
Physical gold demand in India is currently softer, particularly in jewellery and small bars, as higher prices affect price sensitive consumers. However, such periods have historically also created greater interest in financial gold, including ETFs. Gold and silver ETFs remain a small part of India's much larger physical precious metals market, leaving room for further financialisation.
Silver shares many of gold's monetary and diversification characteristics but has an additional industrial demand component. This could make silver structurally attractive, but also more cyclical and volatile. Demand from solar, electronics, electrification and other industrial applications potentially remains an important long term support. At the same time, silver may potentially underperform gold during periods of weaker global growth and typically relatively larger price swings in both directions.
From a portfolio perspective, gold could remain the core precious metals allocation because of its diversification characteristics and relatively lower volatility. Silver may have potential to complement gold as a higher beta exposure to both, precious metals and industrial growth.
For investors, the focus might remain on strategic allocation rather than short term market timing. Given the likelihood that volatility remains elevated, staggered allocation might be preferable after large price moves. Based on historical experience, a minimum investment horizon of two to three years may appropriate, with a longer horizon for silver given its higher volatility.
Overall view the structural case for both metals remains intact, but the path is unlikely to be smooth. Gold may remain supported by high global debt, changing bond market dynamics and diversification demand, while silver might benefit from the same monetary backdrop with additional support from structural industrial demand.
